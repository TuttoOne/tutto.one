AI Fluency for Nonprofits — teaching notes

Side-by-side notes for 12-praxis-ai-fluency-for-nonprofits.html (12 slides). This is the 4D framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) from course 05 reapplied to nonprofit work: donor and grant research, authentic-voice writing, privacy and data hygiene, responsible analysis, workflow augmentation, and organisational policy. The conceptual spine is course 05's; the examples are tuned to a mission-driven room. Written from the deck and 12-ai-fluency-for-nonprofits-lessons.txt, against the Praxis teaching library. Method: teaching-notes-method.md.

How to use these notes
  • Guidance, not a script. Teach the person, not the slides.
  • Every teaching slide has a Pause, then ask block. Put the question, then stop. Don't fill the silence. If they freeze: "Take your time, type anything when you're ready, including 'I don't know'." Then wait again.
  • By background gives a line per archetype (tutor/scenarios.md). Use the one that matches the room; the rest are there so you're never caught without an example. A palette, not a checklist. For a nonprofit room, the strong maps are: Outreach (grants and donor prospecting), Content (donor comms and campaigns), Analyst (giving-data analysis), Exec (the executive director), Form-filler (grant reports against a funder template).
  • This is the same abstract framework as course 05, so the job is the same: keep dragging it back to one real task of theirs, and keep it tied to the mission. The two questions to return to: "what does that mean to you?" and "how does that apply to your work?"
  • Hold the voice (train-the-trainer.md Part 4): no "great question", no emoji, one term — Claude Code. Underclaim. Cost is "about twenty dollars a month" and stop.
  • The privacy slide (Slide 7) is the one that reliably triggers the data fear in this sector. Slow down there; the answers live in objections.md (IT/compliance, privacy).

Title

Working with AI fluently, without losing the mission

The idea

Set the frame: nine ideas, one screen each, and a framework that outlasts the tools. The promise specific to this room is that efficiency never comes at the cost of what the organisation is for.

Say it like this

"Nine ideas, one screen each. This isn't a list of AI tricks that go stale in three months. It's a way of working that stays effective, ethical, and true to your mission whatever the tools do next. I'll show you each idea, then stop and ask where it fits your work."

Open with

The task they named in intake, framed as mission: "We'll run a real piece of your work, a grant, a donor report, a chunk of data, through all four habits by the end, and the whole time we'll ask whether it serves the people you're here for."

Spine

The whole course on one screen

The idea

Foundations (what fluency is, the 4D framework), then the everyday loop (researching, writing), then the bigger calls (privacy, analysis), then making it real (workflow, integration, your first task). Two loops do the work. Orient, don't teach.

Say it like this

"Here's the shape. Two foundations, then four habits split into two loops. Describe and discern is the everyday back-and-forth. Delegate and be diligent is the bigger call about whether AI belongs at all. Then we make it real on something of yours."

Cue

Twenty seconds. The 4D framework on slide 4 is the heart; everything points at it.

Lesson 1

Fluency is four things at once

The idea

AI Fluency is working with AI in a way you'd defend: effective, efficient, ethical, safe. The nonprofit twist on the slide: all four sit on the mission. Every efficiency gain should turn into more impact for the people you serve, not just less work.

Say it like this

"Fluency isn't knowing facts about AI. It's working with it well on four counts at once: effective, efficient, ethical, safe. And for you there's a fifth thing underneath all of them, your mission. The test isn't 'did it save me time', it's 'did the time it saved go back into the people we serve'." (Pairs with framings.md — Claude as collaborator, not a vending machine.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Finish this honestly: 'if AI could help me ____, I'd be able to spend more time ____.' The second blank is the one that matters."
  • "What does it mean to you to use this not just effectively, but ethically and safely?"

(wait)

By background (where the saved time should go)
Investigator
less time pulling records, more time on the judgement the case needs.
Analyst
less time reshaping the giving data, more time on what it means for donors.
Designer
less time on asset variants, more on the creative the campaign deserves.
Knowledge worker
less time on the recurring extraction, more on the exceptions.
Reporter
less time consolidating updates, more time acting on the blockers.
Content
less time drafting, more time on the relationship behind the message.
Form-filler
less time on the grant report's boxes, more on the programme it funds.
Outreach
less time hunting funders, more time building the actual relationship.
Exec
less time on the board pack, more time leading the mission.
Watch for

"Ethical and safe" can sound like box-ticking. In this sector it isn't: it's whose data this is (often vulnerable people), and whether the output respects their dignity. Don't lecture; the slide's own homework is the move, write one reusable context document about the organisation, its mission, your role, your constraints, to paste into future conversations.

Lesson 2

Four competencies, two loops

The centrepiece. If they take one thing away, it's the four Ds and the two loops.

The idea

The 4D framework runs as two loops. Delegation and Diligence are the whether/when loop: should AI touch this at all, and will you own the result. Description and Discernment are the everyday loop: say what you want, judge what comes back, refine. They interlock; you rarely run one without the others.

Say it like this

"Four habits, arranged as two loops. The first loop is the bigger decision: Delegation, should AI even do this, and Diligence, owning the result and being honest about AI's part in it. The second loop is the daily craft: Description, say what you want and how, and Discernment, judge what comes back, then go again. You decide whether AI belongs, then you make the collaboration good. That interplay is the fluency." (Mirrors 05-...-teaching-notes.md slide 5.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Of those four, which do you think you're already weakest at? Most people know."
  • "Take your task: what would delegation even mean for it, before you typed anything?"

(wait)

By background (one task through all four Ds)
Investigator
delegate the record-pulling; describe "cite every source, never infer"; discern the citations hold; stand behind the filing.
Analyst
delegate the giving table; describe the columns and basis; discern the "no data" cells are honest; own the read.
Designer
delegate the variants; describe the brand rules; discern they're on-brand; sign off the campaign.
Knowledge worker
delegate the extraction; describe the schema; discern the flagged rows; trust the output.
Reporter
delegate the consolidation; describe the sections; discern nothing's misattributed; send the briefing.
Content
delegate the draft; describe the house voice; discern it's on-voice and not deficit-framed; publish under your name.
Form-filler
delegate the report's sections; describe the funder's template; discern the totals reconcile; submit it.
Outreach
delegate the per-funder drafts; describe the format; discern no invented numbers; send it.
Exec
delegate the synthesis; describe "facts, not editorial"; discern risks are stated straight; present to the board.
Watch for

Don't let "two loops" become two separate things they run on different days. The slide's caption is the point: the four interlock. You delegate, describe, discern, and stay diligent in one pass on one task. Plant the seed too: "describe what good looks like, once" is exactly the skill-file idea from the Claude courses.

Lesson 3

Research is a loop, not a single ask

The idea

Description and Discernment in their first concrete setting: research. A context-rich prompt (who you serve, what you need) gets you oriented fast. Then you verify claims, notice gaps, check recency, and ask a sharper follow-up. The first answer is a draft. You stay the decision-maker.

Say it like this

"Research with AI isn't one search, it's a loop. You give it real context, who you serve and what you actually need to know, it synthesises, and then the part people skip: you discern. Flag the specific claims that need verifying, notice what's missing for your communities, check it isn't out of date. Then ask again, sharper. It gets you oriented fast, but you stay the one who decides."

Pause, then ask
  • "What's a piece of research you do where being topically close isn't the same as being a real fit, a funder, a partner, a policy?"
  • "Which claim in a research summary would you never act on without verifying yourself?"

(wait)

By background (the research job, and what they'd verify)
Investigator
open-source background on a subject → verify every primary-source claim.
Analyst
benchmarking giving or programme data across sources → check the basis matches.
Designer
a competitor-campaign scan before a pitch → confirm what's current.
Knowledge worker
supplier or partner due-diligence → verify the terms as written.
Reporter
a landscape read to frame the quarter → check recency of each source.
Content
source-gathering for a thought-leadership piece → trace claims to origin.
Form-filler
current thresholds or eligibility before a filing → confirm they're live.
Outreach
donor and grant prospecting → check the funder funds your size and type, and is values-aligned, not just topically related.
Exec
a policy or sector brief for the board → flag what only your network would know.
Watch for

"Confident but wrong" is the failure here. Even when it cites sources, verify the high-stakes ones, ask it to track down the original and compare. (objections.md — hallucinate / make stuff up.) The stretch move from the lesson: ask it for a funder's actual recent grants, not their stated priorities.

Lesson 4

AI drafts faster, you keep the voice

The idea

The same Description-Discernment loop applied to writing. Feed it what it can't know, your track record, partnerships, the way you actually talk, and it drafts fast. Then you discern every line for voice, accuracy, and framing, and you own every word.

Say it like this

"AI can draft a grant proposal in minutes. What it can't do is know your track record, your partnerships, or the way your organisation actually sounds. So you bring that, it drafts from it, and then you judge every line. The trap is vague feedback. 'Make this more compelling' gets you nothing. 'Add our work with the Riverside shelter, in our own plain voice' gets you a draft you can stand behind." (framings.md — brief it like a junior on their first day; spell it out.)

Pause, then ask
  • "What's the context only you could give it, the thing that makes a draft yours and not generic?"
  • "How would you know, line by line, that a draft sounded like your organisation?"

(wait)

By background (the context only they can inject)
Investigator
the house report rules and the standard a filing has to meet.
Analyst
the framing your stakeholders expect around the numbers.
Designer
the brand voice and the campaign's actual story.
Knowledge worker
the supplier quirks and the exceptions that recur.
Reporter
what leadership actually needs to hear this week.
Content
two past posts pasted in for the voice; the real success story behind a post.
Form-filler
the programme detail behind each box on the grant report.
Outreach
the relationship and the track record that make the proposal land.
Exec
the partnerships and outcomes the board already knows are real.
Watch for

Deficit framing about the people you serve. The slide flags it for a reason: AI will reach for "vulnerable", "underprivileged", "needy" by default. Discern it out, and tell it the dignity standard up front. This is exactly the kind of thing only the human in the room would catch, name that as the human-premium moment.

Lesson 5

Decide what AI never needs to see

The idea

The first slide of the Delegation-Diligence loop, and the one the sector worries about most. Most analysis doesn't need names. Work back from the goal to what data is truly necessary, strip what identifies people, match the tool to the sensitivity, then share. Safe use isn't avoiding AI, it's using it responsibly.

Say it like this

"The worry I hear most is 'is our data safe'. Here's the honest answer. First, work backwards from the goal, for pattern analysis you almost never need names or contact details, so strip them, Person A, Donor 1. Second, match the tool to the sensitivity: a free account is fine for brainstorming event themes, anything sensitive wants a protected account with a real retention policy. Safe AI use isn't avoiding it, it's using it on purpose." (objections.md — IT/compliance, privacy. framings.md — no web portal to break, for the "where does it live" version.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Take one real document you'd hesitate to share. What's actually in it that identifies a person, and how much of it does the analysis genuinely need?"
  • "Where's the line in your work between 'fine for a free tool' and 'this needs protection'?"

(wait)

By background (what to strip, and the tool to match)
Investigator
strip the subject's PII you don't need; the most sensitive files want the protected tier or a local model. (objections.md — local options.)
Analyst
anonymise the donor table to ranges and codes before any pattern work.
Designer
brand and brief data is usually low-sensitivity, but check client PII.
Knowledge worker
redact account numbers the extraction doesn't actually need.
Reporter
generalise staff and beneficiary names in the updates before consolidating.
Content
never paste a real beneficiary's identifying story without consent.
Form-filler
the receipts and the grant report may carry PII, match the tier to it.
Outreach
donor contact details rarely belong in the prompt, drop them.
Exec
board inputs are confidential by default, this is the IT/compliance conversation.
Watch for

This is the data-safety gate for almost everyone in the room. Slow down. Give the floor (Claude on the web, no install) and the ceiling (a local model, nothing leaves your network), and for a board-governed organisation frame it as the IT/compliance discovery conversation, with discovery questions to take to them. Concretely: for Claude you adjust privacy settings and opt out of training at privacy.anthropic.com; if something goes wrong, delete the conversation, request deletion, follow your protocols. (objections.md — IT/compliance/privacy. train-the-trainer.md — module 2.)

Lesson 6

Trust it where you can already check it

The idea

The Delegation-Diligence loop made practical: before you let AI loose on new data, test it on past data where you already know the answer. Match the known result with the right guidance, and you've earned the confidence to use it forward. Validation builds trust, it doesn't remove your responsibility.

Say it like this

"The real question with data analysis is 'how do I know I can trust it'. The answer is: don't trust it blind, test it. Run it on data where you already know the right answer. If it matches, you've earned the confidence to use it on new data. If it doesn't, tell it what it missed and go again. And you don't need to be a data person, it writes the formulas and reshapes the mess, as long as you keep asking it to explain so you stay accountable for whether the answer makes sense."

Pause, then ask
  • "What's a piece of analysis where you already know last year's answer, that you could test it against?"
  • "If AI surfaced a pattern in your donor data, what would you check before you believed it?"

(wait)

By background (the known-answer test)
Investigator
run it on a closed matter you know cold; check it reaches your finding.
Analyst
validate against last quarter's giving numbers you've already reconciled.
Designer
test its read on a past campaign whose performance you already know.
Knowledge worker
check its extraction against a month you've already done by hand.
Reporter
see if it consolidates a past week the way you actually wrote it up.
Content
test its pattern-read on posts you already know over- and under-performed.
Form-filler
check its allocation against a filing you've already completed.
Outreach
validate its donor segments against ones you already understand.
Exec
test its board-brief read against a quarter the board already digested.
Watch for

The caption's quiet warning: don't let efficiency override values. If the analysis says "focus on major donors, drop the small ones", that's a recommendation with a community cost, not a neutral fact. Stay transparent about AI's role, and judge the findings against the mission, not just the revenue. The known-answer test is also a clean preview of the skill file: what works in testing is what you write down to replicate.

Lesson 7

"Should AI do this?" beats "Can it?"

The idea

Workflow augmentation, all four Ds at once. Start with Problem Awareness: list what people actually ask before automating anything. Then sort the work into three lanes, AI handles it, AI drafts and you decide, human only. The sort is the whole game. Describe the system in three layers (product, process, performance), then test on real examples.

Say it like this

"The mistake is asking 'can AI do this'. The better question is 'should it'. Start by listing what people actually ask you, the real workload, before you automate a thing. Then sort it: the standardised, documented stuff AI can handle; the things where it drafts and you decide before anything ships; and the things that stay human, complaints, high stakes, judgement calls. The sort is the whole game. Then you describe the system, what it makes, how it works, how it behaves, and test it on real examples until it's right." (Mirrors 05-...-teaching-notes.md slides 8 and 10.)

Pause, then ask
  • "List three things people ask you over and over. Which lane does each fall in, AI handles, AI drafts, or human only?"
  • "What's the one task you'd never let AI run unreviewed, and why?"

(wait — the "human only" lane surfaces the values line; let it.)

By background (a task in each lane)
Investigator
AI handles record-pulling; drafts the report; humans own the conclusion.
Analyst
AI handles the table; drafts the read; humans own what it means.
Designer
AI handles asset variants; drafts concepts; humans own the creative call.
Knowledge worker
AI handles extraction; drafts the summary; humans own exceptions.
Reporter
AI handles consolidation; drafts the briefing; humans own the narrative.
Content
AI handles first drafts; drafts campaign copy; humans own anything personal.
Form-filler
AI handles allocation; drafts the report; humans own the submission.
Outreach
AI handles routine donor replies; drafts proposals; humans own the relationship and any complaint or grievance.
Exec
AI handles synthesis; drafts the brief; humans own every board-facing decision.
Watch for

People over-delegate the judgement and under-delegate the grunt work, the opposite of the win. Steer them: hand over the volume, keep the judgement. And keep all three kinds of diligence visible, be intentional about what you automate (Creation), review outputs before they ship (Deployment), be honest about AI's role (Transparency). (objections.md — permission/auto, for the "let it run" fear.)

Lesson 8

You are the human in the loop

The idea

Integration. Being "the human in the loop" in a mission-driven organisation is more than oversight: you decide what AI should solve, you judge whether it fits your values, you keep the relationships and real-world impact. AI should free you for the handwritten card, not automate it away. A short AI policy lets the whole team work the same way.

Say it like this

"The point of all this isn't to remove the human, it's to free the human for the part only a human can do, the handwritten card, the time with someone in your community. At its best AI clears the noise so you can do more of that. At its worst it automates the human touch away. You're the one in the loop: you choose what AI solves, you judge whether it fits your values, you keep the relationships. And a short policy, what we use, what stays human, who reviews, what we disclose, mission first, lets your whole team work that way, not just one 'AI person'." (framings.md — the system improves through use, for the policy-evolves-as-you-learn point.)

Pause, then ask
  • "What's the human touch in your work you'd protect at all costs, the thing AI should free you for, never replace?"
  • "When you save time with AI, where should that time actually go? Decide it now, not later."

(wait)

By background (the human bit they protect)
Investigator
the judgement call on significance and the conclusion you sign.
Analyst
the interpretation a number can't carry on its own.
Designer
the creative leap and the taste.
Knowledge worker
the handling of the genuinely odd case.
Reporter
reading the room and deciding what leadership needs.
Content
the authentic voice and the real relationship behind it.
Form-filler
the accountability for what's submitted in the organisation's name.
Outreach
the donor relationship and the personal thank-you.
Exec
the mission-level judgement and the accountability you can't delegate.
Watch for

"Will we get too dependent?" The slide's answer is precise: dependency comes from avoidance, not understanding. If you can explain what the AI is doing, that's healthy augmentation; if you can't, rework the process until you can. Also set the norm early about what happens with time saved, so it goes back into human work, not quietly out of the organisation as headcount.

Lesson 9

Run all four Ds on one real task this week

The idea

The close-out exercise: pick something already waiting, a grant, a donor report, data to analyse, and take it round the loop. Delegate, describe, discern, be diligent. It's a loop, not a line, you'll flow back and forth, and that's the fluency growing. AI Fluency is a practice, not a destination.

Say it like this

"Don't leave with theory. Pick one thing already waiting for you this week, a grant, a donor report, a chunk of data, and take it round all four: decide what's AI's and what's yours, describe the context and what you want, discern and refine, then own the result. It's a loop, not a line, you'll go back and change how you delegated halfway through. That back-and-forth is your fluency growing." (framings.md — the system improves through use.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Name the one task you'll run through all four habits this week. One concrete thing, not a category."
  • "What would 'a bit more fluent' look like for you a month from now?"

(wait — their named task is the use case you build the next conversation around.)

one giving-pattern analysis you run each quarter.
Designer
one campaign's full asset list.
Knowledge worker
one month's documents into a clean structured output.
Reporter
this week's briefing from the team's updates.
Content
one post or appeal drafted in house voice from past work.

Close

Delegate. Describe. Discern. Be diligent.

The idea

The whole course in one line: four competencies, two loops, the mission at the centre of every one. Describe and discern day to day; delegate and stay diligent for the bigger calls. The framework keeps working as the models change.

Say it like this

"Four habits, two loops, one anchor. Describe and discern in the everyday back-and-forth. Delegate and stay diligent on the bigger calls about whether AI belongs at all. And the mission sits at the centre of every one. If the tools change next year, and they will, this still holds."

Close with

Their task from slide 11, and the next concrete step.
