Claude 101 — teaching notes

Side-by-side notes for 01-praxis-claude-101.html (15 slides). The deck is what the room looks at; this is what you say, where you stop, and how you make each idea land for the person in front of you. Written from the deck and 01-claude-101-lessons.txt, against the Praxis teaching library. Method: teaching-notes-method.md.

How to use these notes
  • These are guidance, not a script. Teach the person, not the slides.
  • Each teaching slide has a Pause, then ask block. Put the question, then stop. Do not fill the silence. If they freeze, one line: "Take your time, type anything when you're ready, including 'I don't know'." Then wait again.
  • By background gives you a line per archetype (from tutor/scenarios.md). Reach for the one that matches whoever is in the room and ignore the rest. They are a palette, not a checklist to read out.
  • Hold the voice (train-the-trainer.md Part 4): no "great question", no emoji, one term only — Claude Code. Underclaim. Cost is "about twenty dollars a month" and stop.
  • Run the intake five questions first (train-the-trainer.md) so you know which background you're teaching to before slide 1.

Title

What Claude is, and how to actually work with it

The idea

Set the frame: this is twelve ideas, one per screen, and the watching is the lesson. You are not going to lecture; you are going to show, then hand it back to them.

Say it like this

"Twelve ideas, one screen each. I'll show you the thing, then I'll stop and ask you where it fits your work. The point isn't to remember it all today, it's to see it work."

Open with

The intake answer they gave you: name the one task they said eats their week, and promise you'll come back to it on the use-case slide. That is the hook for the whole session.

Spine

The whole course on one screen

The idea

Four moves: meet the tool, learn to talk to it, organise the work, expand its reach and put it to work. Show the shape so nobody feels lost later.

Say it like this

"Here's the whole thing on one screen. We meet Claude, we learn to brief it, we organise so it stops forgetting, then we widen what it can reach. If you lose the thread at any point, this is the map back."

Cue

Don't teach the spine, just orient. Twenty seconds, then move.

Lesson 1

Not a chatbot, a thinking partner

The idea

A chatbot answers and you're on your own. Claude brings the intelligence; you bring the context and judgement; you work the problem together.

Say it like this

"A chatbot is one question in, one answer out, then you're on your own. This is different. It brings the intelligence, you bring the context and the judgement only you have, and you go back and forth until it's right." (Pairs with framings.md — Claude as translator/teacher, and the "brief it like a junior" line.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Where in your week do you already work something out with a colleague, back and forth, rather than just asking one question?"
  • "What's the context or judgement in your job that no chatbot would have?"

(wait)

By background
Investigator
thinking through what a thin case file is missing before you write.
Analyst
deciding whether two vendors can even be compared on the same basis.
Designer
riffing three creative routes for a campaign, not one canned idea.
Knowledge worker
working out how to handle the odd invoice, not just the clean ones.
Reporter
deciding what actually belongs in Monday's briefing and what doesn't.
Content
shaping the argument of a post with you, not generic copy out of a slot.
Form-filler
reasoning through which category an awkward receipt really belongs in.
Outreach
sharpening the angle for one specific client, then drafting together.
Exec
stress-testing the board narrative against the numbers before the meeting.
Watch for

The "is my data safe?" fear often surfaces here, the moment they picture working with it. Don't wave it away. Name it and say you'll cover exactly what it can see on the connectors slide. (objections.md — IT/compliance, "where is the AI".)

Lesson 2

Set the stage · define the task · specify the rules

The centrepiece. If they take one thing away, it's this.

The idea

A good prompt carries three things: who you are and the context (stage), the action you want (task), and the constraints (rules). And the first message starts a conversation, not a one-shot.

Say it like this

"Brief it the way you'd brief a capable new colleague on their first day. Three things: set the stage, who you are and what we're doing; define the task, the actual action; specify the rules, the tone, the format, an example. The mistake everyone makes is being too economical with words. Spell it out." (framings.md — brief like a junior; the six-year-old version for informal rooms. Never use both in one session.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Take that task you said eats your week. Brief me on it out loud, like I'm your new hire. Stage, task, rules."
  • "What's the rule a good person in your job follows that a newcomer wouldn't know yet?"

(wait — let them draft it badly; that's the lesson.)

By background (the same brief, in their words)
Investigator
"You're my due-diligence analyst; summarise this subject's litigation history; cite every source, never infer."
Analyst
"You're reviewing vendor RFIs; build a like-for-like table; mark blanks 'no data'."
Designer
"You're my studio assistant; list every asset this campaign needs; flag missing specs as 'ask client'."
Knowledge worker
"You're my bookkeeper; pull these invoices into a CSV; leave absent fields blank, don't guess."
Reporter
"You're my chief of staff; turn these updates into Monday's briefing; cite who said what."
Content
"You're our staff writer; draft a post in our house voice; reference one past piece."
Form-filler
"You're my tax-prep assistant; allocate each receipt to the template's sections; never drop one."
Outreach
"You're my BD lead; one proposal per customer; only use numbers that are in the file."
Exec
"You're my analyst; one page: where revenue is, what's at risk, what needs a decision."
Watch for

They'll write one vague line and be disappointed. That's the teachable moment, not a failure. Show the difference specificity makes, then set 80/20 before any live run: "You'll get ninety percent of the way; you finish the last ten." (patterns.md — the 80/20 expectation.)

Lesson 3

When it's off, it's almost always fixable

The idea

The first draft is a starting point. Most disappointing answers trace to one thing: context the prompt didn't carry. Too generic, wrong format, confident-but-wrong, tone off — each has a fix.

Say it like this

"When the answer's wrong, the instinct is to think the tool is dumb. Almost always it's that you didn't tell it something you know. Generic? It's missing your role and audience. Wrong shape? Show it an example instead of describing one. Confident but wrong? Ask for sources and verify. This back-and-forth is the skill, and it has a name — AI Fluency, the 4D framework. There's a whole Praxis session on it."

Pause, then ask
  • "When AI has let you down before, which of these four was it — too generic, wrong format, confidently wrong, or wrong tone?"
  • "What would you have needed to tell it to fix that?"

(wait)

By background (the failure they'll hit most, and the fix)
Investigator
confident but wrong on a date or amount → demand a source for every claim.
Analyst
too generic a comparison → hand it the column set and the basis.
Designer
wrong format → show one finished asset as the example.
Knowledge worker
invented a missing VAT number → tell it to leave blanks blank.
Reporter
tone too breezy for leadership → paste a past briefing to match.
Content
off-voice draft → paste two past posts as the voice sample.
Form-filler
mis-filed a receipt → give it the section definitions.
Outreach
generic proposal → feed the customer file, forbid invented numbers.
Exec
editorialised a risk → tell it to state facts, not "at risk".
Watch for

"What if it just makes things up?" is the real fear under this slide. Answer it straight: ask for sources, verify the high-stakes facts, and later the skill file makes "cite a source, never infer" a standing rule. (objections.md — hallucinate / make stuff up.)

Lesson 4

Three modes, matched to the size of the job

The idea

The desktop app gives you one Claude in three shapes — Chat, Cowork, Code — and you pick by how big and how hands-off the work is. Cowork and Code run on Claude Code underneath.

Say it like this

"Same intelligence, three shapes. Chat for quick questions and drafting. Cowork for sustained work across several sources to a finished deliverable. Code for building software. You don't choose a product, you choose by the size of the job."

Pause, then ask
  • "Of the work you do, what's a thirty-second question, and what's a half-day grind across lots of files?"
  • "Which of those would change your week most if it got faster?"

(wait)

By background (where each mode fits)
Investigator
Chat to scope a subject; Cowork to draft the full report from the file.
Analyst
Chat for a definition; Cowork to build the comparison across every response.
Designer
Chat to brainstorm concepts; Cowork to produce the asset manifest.
Knowledge worker
Cowork to process the whole inbox folder in one pass.
Reporter
Cowork to consolidate every update into the weekly briefing.
Content
Chat to test a hook; Cowork to draft against the back catalogue.
Form-filler
Cowork to allocate a full folder of receipts to the template.
Outreach
Cowork to generate one proposal per customer file.
Exec
Chat to pressure-test a number; Code once you want the brief to run itself.
Watch for

This is the bridge to Claude Code without saying "terminal" yet. Default on-ramp is the sidebar, not the terminal (train-the-trainer.md — sidebar first). Don't sell the terminal to someone who hasn't opened the app.

Lesson 5

Projects: a workspace that remembers

The idea

Stop re-uploading the same files and re-explaining the same rules. A project holds knowledge, instructions and memory once; every chat inside it starts already briefed.

Say it like this

"A project is a workspace that remembers. You put the files and the rules in once, and every conversation inside it starts already knowing them. This is the first taste of the big idea: the work stops living in a chat you lose, and starts living in a place that persists." (Previews framings.md — the folder is the memory; the context window. In Claude Code that 'place' becomes a folder on your machine.)

Pause, then ask
  • "What do you find yourself re-explaining to AI every single time you open it?"
  • "If it just knew that, every time, what would you stop repeating?"

(wait)

By background (what goes in the project)
Investigator
the house report rules and screening checklists, briefed into every matter.
Analyst
the standard column set and the "no data" rule for every vendor review.
Designer
brand tokens and retailer spec sheets, ready for any campaign.
Knowledge worker
the CSV schema and known supplier quirks, so each month starts briefed.
Reporter
the five-section briefing format and the team roster.
Content
the house-voice guide and the back catalogue of posts.
Form-filler
the template and the category definitions, reused each cycle.
Outreach
the knowledge base and the proposal format, shared with the team.
Exec
the retainer book and the KPI definitions, so each board prep starts warm.
Watch for

"Does it forget like ChatGPT does?" Yes, a chat does; a project doesn't, and a folder in Claude Code really doesn't. This is the cleanest place to plant why the folder beats the browser. (objections.md — browser / why not ChatGPT.)

Lesson 6

Artifacts: the output, rendered beside the chat

The idea

Not a wall of code buried in the conversation. A finished thing in its own window: rendered, editable, ready to use, and shareable.

Say it like this

"When the thing it makes is significant and reusable, it pops out into its own window beside the chat — a document, a table, a working page — rendered, not buried. If it answers in the chat when you wanted something usable, just say 'make that an artifact'."

Pause, then ask
  • "What's the deliverable you actually hand over — the document, the table, the deck?"
  • "How much of your time goes into formatting that, versus the thinking?"

(wait)

By background (the artifact they'd want rendered)
Investigator
the background report, formatted, ready to lift into the case system.
Analyst
the comparison table, rendered and exportable.
Designer
the asset manifest as a table, plus a CSV.
Knowledge worker
the extracted CSV, viewable and downloadable.
Reporter
the Monday briefing, formatted to drop into email.
Content
the post draft, rendered with headings, ready to paste.
Form-filler
the filled template, laid out to match the form.
Outreach
each proposal, rendered per customer.
Exec
the one-page brief, formatted to print.
Watch for

Iterate one change at a time. People try to fix five things in one message and lose track of which note did what.

Lesson 7

Skills: packaged know-how, run the same way every time

The idea

A skill is a folder of instructions Claude loads when relevant. Two kinds: built-in (Excel, Word, PDF, maintained by Anthropic) and custom (your repeatable workflow, built just by talking). Clean rule: projects store knowledge (the what); skills perform tasks (the how).

Say it like this

"You've already used the built-in skills every time it made you a spreadsheet. The interesting ones are custom: your own repeatable method, captured once and run the same way every time, built just by talking to it — no code. This is the start of the most important idea in the whole programme: the rules your best person carries in their head, written down so the work comes out their way every time." (framings.md — skill file as institutional memory; adapt "senior person" to their role.)

Pause, then ask
  • "What's a task you do the same way every time, where the 'right way' lives in someone's head?"
  • "If that person left tomorrow, what would walk out the door with them?"

(wait — this lands hard for regulated and professional rooms.)

By background (the custom skill they'd build)
Investigator
a Subject Background Report skill (the PI exemplar).
Analyst
a vendor-comparison skill with fixed columns and the "no data" rule.
Designer
an asset-manifest skill that reads briefs and lists every variant.
Knowledge worker
an invoice-to-CSV skill with a review queue for anything ambiguous.
Reporter
a weekly-briefing skill in the five-section format.
Content
a house-voice review skill that flags lines that drift off voice.
Form-filler
a receipt-allocation skill that never drops a line and reconciles totals.
Outreach
a proposal skill, one per customer, in a fixed five sections.
Exec
an exec-brief skill: revenue, at-risk, decisions, steady.
Watch for

Don't conflate two different things both called "skills". The app skill here is one thing; the plain-English skill file you write in Claude Code (course 02, and the deep-dive "Introduction to Agent Skills") is the heart of our method. The deck itself points to that course. Name the difference or they'll merge them. (train-the-trainer.md — module 6 vs module 14.)

Lesson 8

Connectors: bring your tools into the conversation

The idea

Instead of starting cold, Claude works with your actual data — searching, reading and acting across the apps you already use. MCP is the one standard behind it ("USB-C for AI"). Permissions are scoped and revocable; it sees only what you can see.

Say it like this

"Connectors plug Claude into the tools you already live in — Drive, Gmail, Slack and the rest — through one standard. The thing people worry about: it sees only what you can see. Connecting your email doesn't expose anyone else's. You connect what you trust, and you can revoke it."

Pause, then ask
  • "Where does the data you'd want it to work with actually live today?"
  • "Which one tool, connected, would save you the most copy-paste?"

(wait)

By background (the connector that earns its place)
Investigator
Drive where case files live — after the IT/compliance conversation.
Analyst
the warehouse or Drive holding vendor responses.
Designer
Adobe or Figma, plus Drive for brand assets.
Knowledge worker
Gmail and Drive where the invoices land.
Reporter
Slack and Drive where the status updates live.
Content
the CMS or Notion holding the back catalogue.
Form-filler
Drive or email where receipts arrive.
Outreach
the CRM and Gmail for customer context.
Exec
Drive, Slack and email for the board-pack inputs.
Watch for

This is the data-safety gate for almost everyone. Slow down. Give the floor (Claude on the web, no install) and the ceiling (a local model, nothing leaves your network), and for banks and firms frame it as the IT/compliance discovery conversation. (objections.md — IT/compliance/privacy. train-the-trainer.md — module 2.)

Lesson 9

Enterprise Search: ask your whole company one question

The idea

A pre-built project over your organisation's knowledge. "What happened while I was out?" answered across every connected source, with citations. Team and Enterprise plans only, and it returns only what you already have permission to see.

Say it like this

"Imagine asking your whole company one question and getting one answer, drawn from the docs, the chat and the email you already have access to, with citations back to the source. That's Enterprise Search. An admin sets it up; you only ever see what you're already allowed to."

Pause, then ask
  • "What question do you ask three different people or systems to answer today?"
  • "What would you ask your whole organisation, if you could ask it once?"

(wait)

By background (the "while I was out" question)
Investigator
"what's the latest across all open matters?"
Analyst
"which vendor reviews are still missing data?"
Designer
"where's the approved master for this campaign?"
Knowledge worker
"which suppliers changed terms this quarter?"
Reporter
"what shipped across all squads last week?" — the briefing, org-wide.
Content
"what have we already published on this topic?"
Form-filler
"what's still outstanding for this filing period?"
Outreach
"what's the status of every deal in this segment?"
Exec
"what happened while I was out?", answered across the whole company.
Watch for

Be honest about the plan gate: a solo operator or small firm won't have this yet. Don't oversell a feature they can't switch on. Frame it as where this goes at team scale, then return to what they can do today.

Lesson 10

Research: from one search to a researched report

The idea

Not a single lookup. Claude works agentically: it plans, runs many searches that build on each other, then writes it up with every claim cited. Minutes, not seconds.

Say it like this

"Research isn't one search, it's many — it plans, searches, decides what to look for next based on what it found, then writes it up with citations. Most reports land in five to fifteen minutes. You reach for this when a good answer needs many sources, not one."

Pause, then ask
  • "What's a piece of digging you do that means opening twenty tabs and pulling it together?"
  • "Where would a cited, written-up version of that save you an afternoon?"

(wait)

By background (the research job)
Investigator
open-source background research on a subject, every claim cited.
Analyst
market-rate benchmarking across many sources for a vendor review.
Designer
a competitor-campaign scan before a pitch.
Knowledge worker
supplier due-diligence before onboarding.
Reporter
a landscape read to frame the quarterly narrative.
Content
source-gathering for a thought-leadership piece, cited.
Form-filler
checking the current thresholds or allowances before a filing.
Outreach
a prospect or company brief before writing the proposal.
Exec
a market or competitor brief for the board, with citations.
Watch for

Tie back to slide 5: the cited report is how you catch "confident but wrong". Verify the high-stakes facts even when it shows its sources. (objections.md — hallucinate.)

Lesson 11

Whatever your role, there's a starting point

The idea

The point isn't the feature list, it's the work. There's one concrete place to begin in every function. This is where you return to the task they named in intake.

Say it like this

"Forget the feature list. The only thing that matters is the one task you'd start with this week. Not a category — one concrete thing you'll actually do. Let's name yours." (Then go to their task from intake, not the grid on the slide.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Of everything we've seen, what's the one task you'd point this at first?"
  • "What does 'good' look like for that task, so you'd know it worked?"

(wait — their answer is the use case you build the rest of the relationship around.)

By background (the one starting task)
Investigator
turn one case file into a structured background report.
Analyst
one vendor comparison you run every quarter.
Designer
one campaign's full asset list.
Knowledge worker
one month's invoices into a clean CSV.
Reporter
this week's briefing from the team's updates.
Content
one post drafted in house voice from the back catalogue.
Form-filler
one filing period's receipts allocated to the template.
Outreach
one segment's proposals, personalised.
Exec
one board prep turned into a one-page brief.
Watch for

Resist the urge to add features. The win is one task done, not ten shown. The next session is hands-on with their files, not more theory. (patterns.md — set the next concrete step.)

Lesson 12

One intelligence, where you already work

The idea

claude.ai is one front door. The same Claude shows up inside the tools you spend your day in — Claude Code, Slack, Excel, PowerPoint, Chrome — each shape tuned to that surface. Where you go next depends on the work.

Say it like this

"This is one front door, not the only one. The same intelligence turns up in the terminal and your editor as Claude Code, in Slack, in Excel and PowerPoint, in the browser. You don't go to it; it meets you where the work already happens." (Hold the one term: it's Claude Code, every time.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Which tool do you spend the most hours in each week?"
  • "What would change if the intelligence we've been using lived inside that tool?"

(wait)

Excel sidebar for models; Claude Code for the repeatable comparison.

the design surfaces, plus Claude Code for batch asset generation.
Knowledge worker
Claude Code on the folder, so the monthly job becomes a script.

Close

Talk to it like a colleague. Give it your context. Iterate to the result.

The idea

The whole course in one line: brief it like a colleague (stage, task, rules), then refine. Projects, artifacts and skills organise the work; connectors, search and research expand its reach; and it meets you in the tools you already use.

Say it like this

"If you keep one thing: talk to it like a capable colleague, give it the context only you have, and iterate to the result. Everything else today was a way to do that better, or in more places."

Close with

Their task from slide 13, and the next concrete step: "Next time we open your real folder and build the one you named." Then the session-improvement habit: "at the end, tell it to look back at what we did badly today and save how we fixed it." (patterns.md — close every session by teaching the system to improve.)
