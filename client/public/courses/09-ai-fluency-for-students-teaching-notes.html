AI Fluency for Students — teaching notes

Side-by-side notes for 09-praxis-ai-fluency-for-students.html (8 slides). This course reapplies the 4D framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) — the same spine taught in full in course 05 (AI Fluency Foundations) — to studying, learning, and early career. The angle that runs through every slide: augment, don't automate, because in learning the doing is the point. Written from the deck and 09-ai-fluency-for-students-all-lessons.html (no lessons.txt exists for this course), against the Praxis teaching library. Method: teaching-notes-method.md.

How to use these notes
  • Guidance, not a script. Teach the person, not the slides.
  • Every teaching slide has a Pause, then ask block. Put the question, then stop. Don't fill the silence. If they freeze: "Take your time, type anything when you're ready, including 'I don't know'." Then wait again.
  • By background gives a line per archetype (tutor/scenarios.md). Use the one that matches the room; the rest are there so you're never caught without an example. A palette, not a checklist.
  • This course's whole argument is augmentation over automation. Keep dragging it back to one real thing they're trying to learn or get better at, not just produce. The two questions to keep returning to: "what does that mean to you?" and "how does that apply to your work?"
  • Hold the voice (train-the-trainer.md Part 4): no "great question", no emoji, one term — Claude Code. Underclaim. Cost is "about twenty dollars a month" and stop.

How to use AI and still do the learning

The idea

Set the frame: enduring judgement, not prompt tricks. Five ideas, one screen each. The promise in the headline is the whole course: you can use AI heavily and come out stronger, not hollowed out — but only if you choose the right way to use it.

Say it like this

"The fear with AI in any kind of learning is that you press a button, the work gets done, and you learn nothing. This course is the opposite of that. Five ideas, one screen each. By the end you'll know how to use AI so that you end up sharper, not dependent. The watching is the lesson, so I'll stop a lot and hand it back to you."

Open with

Whatever they said in intake they're trying to get better at — a tool, a qualification, a skill, an interview. "We'll keep coming back to that one thing."

Lesson 1

Augmentation, not automation

The idea

Two ways to use AI. Automation: it does the work, the task is done, you're no further on. Augmentation: it helps you do the work better and you grow in the doing. When the point is the output, automate freely. When the point is that you end up more capable, augment. In learning, only augmentation leaves you stronger. Fluency is using AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely — and knowing which of those two you're choosing.

Say it like this

"Two modes, and they feel identical from the outside. Automation: the AI does it for you, the thing gets made, but you didn't change. Augmentation: it helps you do it, and you come out the other side better at it. Neither is wrong. The skill is knowing which one the moment calls for. If you're trying to actually learn this thing, the shortcut that automates it away robs you. These are durable principles, not prompt tricks — they hold however the tools change." (Pairs with the framings spine in course 05, slide 4 — automation / augmentation / agency.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Think of one thing you're trying to get better at right now. Are you currently automating it or augmenting it? Be honest."
  • "Where in your week is the output the only thing that matters — and where is it actually you getting sharper that matters?"

(wait — the second question is the whole course; let it sit.)

By background (automate the output vs augment the learning)
Investigator
automate the boilerplate of a report; augment when you're learning to read a new kind of evidence.
Analyst
automate the table you've built a hundred times; augment when you're learning a method that's new to you.
Designer
automate batch resizing; augment when you're developing a craft you don't have yet.
Knowledge worker
automate the monthly extraction; augment when the process itself is what you need to understand.
Reporter
automate the consolidation; augment when you're learning what leadership actually needs to hear.
Content
automate the formatting; augment when you're finding your own voice.
Form-filler
automate the allocation; augment when the rules are new and you need to internalise them.
Outreach
automate the merge; augment when you're learning to read a market.
Exec
automate the recurring brief; augment when you're getting your head around a domain you'll be accountable for.
Watch for

The job-loss / deskilling fear lives right here ("so it does my thinking for me?"). Name it and answer with the slide's own logic: automation is fine where you don't need to grow, and augmentation is exactly how you stay irreplaceable. A person who knows which mode to pick beats both the pure automator and the AI alone.

Lesson 2

The 4D Framework

The centrepiece. If they take one thing away, it's the four Ds.

The idea

Four habits that make any AI interaction sound. Delegation: decide what to hand off, and what not to. Description: say what you want — product, process, performance. Discernment: judge what comes back — is this actually any good? Diligence: own the result, responsible and transparent. Delegation and Diligence bookend the work; Description and Discernment are the engine in between, a loop you run on repeat until it's right. These hold no matter how the technology evolves.

Say it like this

"Four habits. Delegation: what's even worth handing over, and what stays yours. Description: say what you want — what to make, how to approach it, how it should behave with you. Discernment: judge what comes back, honestly. Diligence: own whatever you submit. The middle two are a loop — describe, look, describe better, look again. Delegation sets it up, Diligence keeps it honest. That's the whole framework, and it's the same four whether you're studying for an exam or running a department." (Same spine as course 05, slide 5 — the four Ds.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Of those four, which one do you think you're already weakest at? Most people know."
  • "Take the thing you're trying to learn — what would delegation even mean for it before you typed anything? What would you refuse to hand over?"

(wait)

By background (one learning task through all four Ds)
Investigator
delegate the background reading; describe "teach me, don't conclude for me"; discern whether you could now brief a colleague; stand behind your own judgement.
Analyst
delegate practice problems; describe the method you're trying to grasp; discern whether you can do one unaided; own the answer in the review.
Designer
delegate references and critique; describe the craft you're building; discern whether your eye improved; ship work that's genuinely yours.
Knowledge worker
delegate worked examples; describe the process to learn; discern you could run it without prompts; own the output.
Reporter
delegate the drafting drills; describe the standard you're after; discern the briefing reads as yours; send it under your name.
Content
delegate idea-pressure-testing; describe your voice; discern it still sounds like you; publish it.
Form-filler
delegate practice cases; describe the rules to internalise; discern you can allocate unaided; submit it accountably.
Outreach
delegate research and rehearsal; describe the angle you're learning to find; discern the pitch is authentically yours; send it.
Exec
delegate the briefing of a new domain; describe "explain it so I can defend it"; discern you could answer a board question on it; own the call.
Watch for

"Describe what good looks like, once" is the seed of the Praxis skill file — the same idea taught in Claude 101 and Agent Skills. With professionals you can plant it: the description layer, written down, is institutional memory. Don't dwell; the next two slides make it concrete.

Lesson 3

A coach, not a substitute player

The idea

The same prompt can do your homework or build your understanding — the difference is how you steer it. Configure AI to question, hint and check rather than hand over answers, and you stay in the driver's seat: you try, it prompts, you explain it in your own words, you check whether it holds. The acid test is simple: could you explain it with no AI in the room? Two tools come out of this lesson — a study buddy set to question, not answer, and a living journal that tracks what actually clicked.

Say it like this

"Here's the move that decides whether AI makes you smarter or dependent. A substitute player goes onto the pitch and plays for you — you sit on the bench and learn nothing. A coach stands on the touchline, asks you the hard question, makes you do the rep yourself. Same AI, you just steer it differently: 'don't give me the answer, ask me questions until I get there.' The test for whether it worked: could you explain it in a room with no AI? That's the only test that counts." (The "stay in the driver's seat, AI coaches, it doesn't play for you" line is from the lesson video; use it verbatim.)

Pause, then ask
  • "When you've used AI to learn something, did you come away able to explain it — or just with the task done?"
  • "What's one thing you'd want a coach to drill you on, rather than just hand you?"

(wait — this is the human-premium moment for this course; let them feel the difference.)

By background (what they'd set the study buddy to drill)
Investigator
"quiz me on why this source is primary or reported — don't tell me."
Analyst
"give me a problem, hints only, until I can run the method myself."
Designer
"critique my draft with questions, don't redesign it for me."
Knowledge worker
"make me explain each step of the process back to you."
Reporter
"ask me what belongs in this briefing and push back on my reasoning."
Content
"interrogate my argument until I can defend it in my own words."
Form-filler
"test me on which section this belongs in and why, progressively harder."
Outreach
"play the sceptical buyer and make me answer, don't script it for me."
Exec
"explain this domain by asking me questions until I could brief the board on it."
Watch for

The pull to just take the answer is strong, especially under time pressure — people will say "but it's faster to let it do it". Agree, then reframe: faster today, weaker in the exam, the interview, the meeting where there's no AI. The harder path is the one that builds capability you keep. (This is the augmentation argument from slide 3, made concrete.)

Lesson 4

Information from AI, authenticity from you

The idea

Use AI to research, structure and rehearse — never to manufacture a version of you. The CV-to-interview workflow: critique your CV against the spec, let AI draw out your real examples, revise your experience sharper, then rehearse a mock interview and take the feedback. AI brings the research and the drills; you bring the real experience and your own voice. Employers spot generic AI output instantly — so every word that represents you stays yours. And AI fluency itself is now a skill worth showing.

Say it like this

"There's a clean line here. AI is brilliant at the information side: researching roles, structuring a CV against a spec, running you through mock questions. It's poison the moment it starts inventing a version of you — because the people on the other side can smell generic AI output from across the room, and because the person who shows up to the interview has to be the real one. So the workflow uses AI to draw out your actual examples and sharpen how you tell them. The experience is yours. The voice is yours. The AI just helps you say it better." (Mirrors course 05, slide 6 — generate, not retrieve: the research is the AI's, the truth of who you are is yours.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Where could AI genuinely help you prepare for something — research, structure, practice — without ever speaking for you?"
  • "What's the part of how you represent yourself that has to stay one hundred percent yours?"

Watch for

Someone will ask "so can it just write my cover letter / proposal / bio?" — it can, and that's exactly the trap. Answer straight: it can draft, but generic AI output is recognised and discounted, so the value is using it to surface and sharpen what's true about you, not to fabricate. And name the upside: being visibly fluent with AI is itself a credential now.

Lesson 5

You're the one in the loop

The idea

Being "the human in the loop" means you stay the decision-maker: you decide what to ask, you judge whether the answer holds, and you bring the values a model can't. This isn't a one-off — it's a practice. Write it down as a personal AI policy you'll actually follow: when you will and won't use it, how you use it to learn, what you'll disclose, which skills you keep sharp, your ethical lines, how you keep growing. Your judgement, creativity and care stay irreplaceable.

Say it like this

"Everything we've done points at one person: you, in the loop. You choose what to ask. You judge whether what comes back actually holds up. You bring the values — the model has none of its own. The way you make that durable instead of a vague intention is to write it down: a short personal policy. When I'll use AI and when I won't. How I use it to learn rather than skip learning. What I'll be open about. Which skills I keep sharp by hand. My red lines. Six lines, and you'll actually follow them because you wrote them." (The "write the rules down once" move is the same instinct as the skill file — framings.md, skill file as institutional memory; here the institution is you.)

Pause, then ask
  • "What's one red line for you — a place you'd never let AI do the work, full stop?"
  • "If you wrote your own AI policy in six lines, what would the first line be?"

(wait — their first line is the start of a real document; treat it as such.)

By background (the policy line that matters most to them)
Investigator
"I verify every AI-surfaced fact against a primary source before it goes in a report."
Analyst
"I never present a number I couldn't reproduce and defend myself."
Designer
"the concept is mine; AI supplies volume and reference, never the idea."
Knowledge worker
"I keep the process in my own head, not just in the script."
Reporter
"the judgement of what matters stays mine; AI only consolidates."
Content
"nothing publishes under my name that I couldn't have written in my voice."
Form-filler
"I stay current on the rules myself; I don't outsource knowing them."
Outreach
"I never let AI claim a relationship or a result that isn't real."
Exec
"I own every decision I present, AI-assisted or not — accountability doesn't delegate."
Watch for

This is the slide that justifies the human teacher over the video, and the human professional over the AI. Name it plainly: discernment and values don't automate away. It's also the natural home for the disclosure and transparency conversation — for regulated rooms, link it to who needs to know AI was involved. (objections.md — IT/compliance.) End with the practice habit: keep asking is this helping me grow, are my skills sharpening, am I being honest about its role? — the system, and you, improve through use (patterns.md).

Close

Augment, don't automate. Coach, don't outsource. Stay in the loop.

The idea

The whole course in three lines: the 4Ds are the frame, the learning and career workflows are the frame in practice, the human in the loop is the standard. Choose augmentation when the doing is the point, steer AI to coach rather than play for you, and keep the judgement, the voice and the accountability yours. If the tools change next year, this still holds.

Say it like this

"Three lines. Augment, don't automate — when the point is you getting stronger. Coach, don't outsource — make it ask, don't let it answer for you. Stay in the loop — the judgement and the values are yours to keep. The tools will change. This won't."
