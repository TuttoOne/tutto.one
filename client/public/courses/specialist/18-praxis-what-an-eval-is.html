Tutto.Praxis · talking deck
Praxis — Advanced track · 45 minutes

What an eval is,
and when you
need one.

The theory before the workshop. What these things actually are, why the first one people build almost always gets abandoned, and how to pick the right kind for the work in front of you.

1
Speaker notes

This deck is the theory session. It pairs with Measuring extraction, the 75-minute workshop that follows. Run this first if the room has never measured anything, or skip it and go straight to the workshop if they have.

Forty-five minutes at a comfortable pace, thirty if you cut slides 8 and 9.

Do not use the extraction example in detail here. The workshop depends on the learner discovering the reveal for themselves, and a worked walkthrough now spoils it.

Speaker notes

Do not linger. Thirty seconds. The spine exists so nobody spends the session wondering how long it runs.

If someone asks up front "is this just for developers" — no, and say why now rather than later: the hard part of an eval is deciding what counts as correct, and that is a domain judgment, not a technical one. The code is twenty lines.

Tutto.1 · What
The problem

You checked it once, and it looked fine

That is a demo. It tells you the tool can work. It does not tell you how often it does.

3
Speaker notes

The whole session hangs off the difference between a demo and a rate. Say it in those words.

Useful question to the room: "how do you currently know your process is working?" Most answers are a version of "someone would notice". Let that sit for a second before moving on. Do not mock it — it is a reasonable answer for low-volume work, and evals only earn their keep when volume or stakes rise.

If a room is sceptical, the strongest argument is not accuracy, it is change. You cannot safely edit a prompt or a rule without a way to tell whether the edit helped.

Tutto.2 · What
Where the line sits

Tests for the boring parts. Evals for the judgment.

Two tools, one line between them. Most confusion about evals is someone reaching for the wrong side of it.

4
Speaker notes

This is the slide people remember. If they leave with only one thing, make it this line.

Anyone who has done Lab 01, Tests already knows the left-hand side. Position the eval as the second tool that sits on the same line, not as a replacement. If nobody in the room has done Tests, do not mention Lab 01 at all — it lands as jargon.

Common objection: "can't I just write more tests?" Answer with an example from their own work where two different outputs would both be acceptable. A test cannot express "either of these is fine". That is the whole reason the right-hand column exists.

Tutto.3 · Which kind
The choice that decides everything else

Does the question have one right answer, or several good ones?

Answer that and the kind of eval is already decided. Get it wrong and you will spend a week building the wrong instrument.

5
Speaker notes

Get the room to sort their own work into the two columns out loud. Two or three examples is enough. This is the most useful ninety seconds in the deck.

Watch for the answer that sits on the line — "extract the key risks from this report". That is judgment wearing extraction's clothes, because two readers would pick different risks. Name it when it comes up rather than avoiding it; the ambiguity is real and spotting it is the skill.

The workshop that follows is answer key only. Rubrics are Lab 07. Say which one they are about to do so nobody expects the other.

Tutto.4 · Which kind
How the scoring actually happens

Four ways to grade, cheapest first

Use the cheapest one that can express what you mean. Reaching for the expensive one first is the most common mistake.

6
Speaker notes

The line that matters here is calibration: before you trust a model judge, grade twenty cases by hand as well and see whether the two agree. If they disagree badly, the criteria are vague, not the judge.

Most rooms assume they need option 3 and actually need option 2. The extraction workshop is entirely option 2, and the low score people get on their first run is almost always option 1 applied by accident.

If someone asks about cost: the graders are not where the money goes. Running an eval is about twenty dollars a month of usage. The cost is the day someone spends building the case set, which is slide 9.

Tutto.5 · How
Anatomy

Every eval is the same four parts

Whatever the kind, whatever the domain. If one of these is missing you do not have an eval, you have a spreadsheet.

7
Speaker notes

"Frozen, not live data" is worth a sentence. If the case set changes underneath you, the number is not comparable week to week and the whole exercise is decorative.

The last line is the one that reframes who this is for. Say it plainly: the bottleneck is the person who owns the standard, not the person who can code. In most firms those are different people, and the eval fails when the second one guesses on behalf of the first.

Three to five cases is enough to start. People assume they need hundreds and never begin.

Tutto.6 · How
What you actually do with it

The number is not a grade. It is a direction.

Nobody needs to know they are at 73%. What they need is to know whether yesterday's change helped.

8
Speaker notes

Fix the specification, never the output. This is the discipline the workshop enforces and it is worth planting here so it is a second hearing, not a first.

Correcting an output fixes one cell today and nothing tomorrow. Editing the answer key to match your output is marking your own homework — say that phrase, it stops the behaviour better than an explanation.

The closing line is the most defensible claim in the whole subject. An eval does not tell you the work is right. It tells you where to look.

Tutto.7 · What goes wrong
Why most first evals get abandoned

Three ways this goes wrong

People build one, get a bad number, conclude the tool cannot do the job, and stop. The number was usually measuring the wrong thing.

9
Speaker notes

The first one is the reason the workshop exists. Do not over-explain it here — in the workshop they will hit it live and it teaches far better than a slide does. One sentence, move on.

The second one is a governance point, not a technical one. Whoever owns the key should not be the person whose work is being scored by it. Worth saying in a corporate room; usually lands.

The third is the argument for looking at a scorecard rather than a headline. If someone asks how to catch regression properly, the true answer is per-field tracking over time, and that is beyond both of these sessions. Say so rather than improvising.

Tutto.8 · What goes wrong
Before you promise this at work

Cheap to run. Expensive to start.

The cost falls the opposite way round to what most people assume, and getting it wrong is how these die in month two.

10
Speaker notes

Say the day out loud. Trainers who skip this send people back to the office with a false estimate, and the first thing that happens is someone promises an eval by Friday.

The corollary is the useful one for choosing where to start: an eval pays off on work that repeats. High volume, or high stakes, or a process that several people run differently. A one-off analysis does not need one.

If asked about tooling cost, keep it to usage: about twenty dollars a month. Do not get drawn into platform comparisons; the whole thing is a folder and a script.

Tutto.What's next
Where this goes

Two sessions, one for each half

You now have the map. The next two sessions are the two halves of it, built by hand.

11
Speaker notes

Set up the workshop without spoiling it. Tell them what they will build — a schema, an extraction skill, an accuracy number and a way to move it. Do not tell them that the schema turns out to be the grader. That discovery is the spine of the next 75 minutes.

If the room will only ever do one of the two, the extraction workshop is the better choice for most commercial work. Rubrics matter more for content and advisory teams.

Break here if you are running both back to back. Ten minutes.

Tutto.Praxis
The whole thing, in one line

An eval does not prove
you are right. It tells you
which way you moved.

Write down what counts as correct. Freeze a small set of cases. Score them the cheapest way that expresses what you mean. Then change a rule and watch the number.

12
Speaker notes

Do not add to this. The four sentences in the lead are the session, and anything after them dilutes it.

If there is time for questions, the two that come up most are "how many cases is enough" (nobody knows, three to five to start, more when the number stops surprising you) and "who signs off the key" (whoever owns the standard, and it must not be the person being measured).
