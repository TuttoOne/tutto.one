Tutto.Praxis · talking deck
Praxis — Advanced track · 75 minutes

Measuring
extraction.

You will pull the same fields out of four contracts, and then answer a harder question: how often was it right? Not "does that look correct". A number.

1
Speaker notes

Seventy-five minutes. A 60-minute cut is in the notes on slide 2. Pairs with the theory deck, What an eval is — run that first if the room has never measured anything.

Prerequisite is a completed Plato build. Lab 07 is not required; if they have done it, position the two directly: a rubric grades work with no single right answer, an answer key grades work with exactly one. If they have not done Lab 07, do not mention it.

Check before you start: the answer key and the completed schema are in a folder you have told them not to open. If someone opens the key during Part 4, the rest of the class is theatre.

Speaker notes

Thirty seconds. Do not sell the arc.

The 60-minute cut: pre-fill six schema rows instead of three (Part 1 drops to 8 min), and demo a prebuilt scorecard rather than building it live (Part 6 drops to 3 min). Cut nothing else. Parts 4, 5 and 7 are the class; everything else is scaffolding for them.

If the room wants to skip straight to the tool, run Part 4 early. The 340-value wall answers that objection better than you can.

Tutto.Setup
What you are working with

Four managed-services agreements

Twenty to thirty pages each, written the way a real supplier contract is written. Every sector signs these: law, accounting, financial services, personal injury.

3
Speaker notes

The terms differ on purpose. A learner who notices all four run 36 months starts pattern-matching instead of reading, and the traps stop working.

Do not walk the documents. They are long and the room does not need a tour. Point at the folder and move on.

Tutto.Part 1 · Build · 15 min
Exercise 1 · 15 minutes

Finish the schema

A schema is the formal definition of the data you want out. Three things for every field.

"Contract value" sounds obvious until you open the document and find three numbers that all have a claim to it. Write down which one you mean.

4
Speaker notes

Must not say: that the match rule column is the grader, that this is an eval, or that any of this will be scored later. If asked why the third column matters, answer with the contract-value example and nothing more.

Watch for the learner who writes "the correct value" in the match rule column. Push once: correct according to what? That is the whole exercise.

Fill in the first two or three fields together, out loud, then let them work. Fifteen minutes feels long and is not.

Tutto.Part 2 · Build · 10 min
Exercise 2 · 10 minutes

Extract one agreement

Point Claude Code at your schema and at the Northgate agreement. One row per field, following the match rule column.

Look it over. Does anything seem off to you?

Hold that. Do not fix it yet. We will come back to it with a number instead of an opinion.

5
Speaker notes

Must not say: which field is wrong, or how to fix it.

Somebody will want to correct a field right now. Do not let them. The point of the class is that the fix comes from evidence, and there is no evidence yet. The two lines on the slide are the whole intervention — read them and stop.

This is where you plant the doubt. If nobody says anything is off, that is fine too. Move on.

Tutto.Part 3 · Build · 5 min
Exercise 3 · 5 minutes

Package it as a skill

Turn the working prompt into a file so you can run it again without retyping. Ask Claude Code to write it, and to ask you clarifying questions first.

Nothing clever. The reason we are saving it now is that in ten minutes you are going to want to run this same extraction four times, and then run it again after you change it.

6
Speaker notes

Must not say: why they are going to need to re-run it. That gives away Part 4.

Five minutes, and it is genuinely mechanical. Have them ask Claude Code to write the skill and to ask clarifying questions first, then review what it proposes before saving.

Tutto.Part 4 · Run wide · 10 min
Exercise 4 · 10 minutes

Four agreements, full schedules

Run the skill across all four. One record per agreement, plus the complete charge schedule for each. Not a sample. All of it.

Try eyeballing that. Tell me your error rate.

7
Speaker notes

Say as little as possible. Ask the question on the slide and then let the silence sit. Nobody can answer it. That is the answer.

If someone starts scrolling and squinting, let them scroll for a while before you move on. The discomfort is the teaching, and cutting it short costs you the next twenty minutes.

Only once the room has felt it, name the gap: eyeballing one result is fine for a demo, in production you need a rate you can compare against last week's.

Tutto.Part 4 · Run wide
What we do instead

Somebody wrote down the right answer for every field

By hand, checking each value against the document. That is the answer key.

8
Speaker notes

Say the cost out loud. "Most of a day" is the most useful sentence in this class for anyone deciding whether to do this at work. Trainers who skip it send people back to the office with a false estimate.

85% of the gradeable surface is the charge schedule. That is deliberate: it puts the number where the real differences hide, and it makes eyeballing physically impossible.

If asked what it costs to run: about twenty dollars a month.

Tutto.Part 5 · Grade · 5 min
Exercise 5.1 · 5 minutes

Score it against the key

Ask Claude Code to compare its output to the answer key, field by field, and report where it matches and where it misses.

Pick one of the failures and read both values out to me.

9
Speaker notes

Do not warn anyone about formatting. Let the number come back low. Expect something in the 40s or 50s.

Every date fails on the format collision, amounts fail on currency symbols and separators, "Ltd" fails against "Limited". The extraction is mostly right and the score says it is mostly wrong. That gap is the lesson.

Someone will read out £659,400.00 against 659400. Let the room land on it themselves rather than explaining it.

If a sharp learner spotted the date collision back in Part 1: agree immediately. "You are right, they disagree. They were built by different people at different times, and that is normal. The data agrees. The formatting does not. Which of those is the eval supposed to be measuring?"

Tutto.Part 5 · Grade · 5 min
Exercise 5.2 · 5 minutes

Normalise, then score again

A correct answer can look different from the answer key. Strip the formatting off both sides before you compare.

Normalise the formatting on both sides before you score, or you will grade the punctuation instead of the data.

10
Speaker notes

Deliver the line on the slide verbatim. It is the single most reusable sentence in the class and the reason most first evals get abandoned. Slow down for it.

Grade the charge schedule period by period, not as a block. That is where the real differences hide.

The number on the slide is illustrative. Use whatever your run actually produces.

Tutto.Part 5 · The reveal
Exercise 5.3 — if you take one thing away

You wrote the grader this morning

Everything still failing after normalisation traces back to one field whose match rule was vague, or blank. Open the schema you filled in an hour ago.

You did not write a data definition this morning. You wrote the grader, and then you wrote a tool that gets graded by it.

The specification and the answer key are the same document.

11
Speaker notes

This is the beat the class was built around.

Open the schema from Part 1 next to the results before you say anything. Everything still failing after normalisation traces to a field whose match rule was vague or blank. Let them see that first.

Then deliver the two lines. Then stop talking. Do not restate it, do not summarise it, do not add an example. Let them get there.

Tutto.Part 6 · Act · 8 min
Exercise 6 · 8 minutes

Build a scorecard

A wall of pass and fail text is technically an eval and practically useless. You want to look at this and know within three seconds which field is your problem.

12
Speaker notes

In the 60-minute cut, show a prebuilt scorecard instead of building one. Three minutes.

The artefact matters more than the exercise: this is what you would put in front of a partner or a board. A wall of pass and fail text is technically an eval and practically useless.

A whole row failing is one missing rule, not four mistakes. Make that read off the picture rather than saying it.

Tutto.Part 7 · Act · 8 min
Exercise 7 · 8 minutes

Fix the specification, never the output

Take the top failing field. Work out which of three things it is, change the rule, and re-run the whole thing.

Correcting the answer fixes one cell today and nothing tomorrow. You change the rule and re-run. If the number goes up, the rule was the problem. If it goes down, you have learned something about your own thinking.

13
Speaker notes

The most common derail in the whole class. Someone will edit the output spreadsheet, or worse, edit the answer key to match their output. Stop it immediately and name it: that is marking your own homework. The key does not move.

Take the top failing field only. One diagnosis, one fix, one re-run. Watching the number move is the payoff; a second example adds nothing.

Tutto.Part 8 · The close · 4 min
Part 8

What this does not give you

A number you trust too much is worse than no number. Five things this class did not teach, and you should know all of them.

What it gives you is direction. Change a rule, and within a minute you know whether it helped or hurt. That is enough to stop guessing, and stopping guessing is most of the value.

14
Speaker notes

Name all five. A trainer who leaves them out sends people away thinking evals are a solved problem, and the first confident wrong number costs more than the class was worth.

The one that matters commercially is the first: a senior person spends a day building the key, and that day is the actual barrier to entry. Everything else is cheap.

Do not oversell. An eval does not prove the output is correct. It proves the output agrees with a key that a person wrote, and that person can be wrong.

Write down what counts
as correct, and the
eval comes free.

An answer key grades extraction, where one value is right. A rubric grades judgment, where several outputs could be good. Between them you can measure both halves of the work. Neither one measures the other.
