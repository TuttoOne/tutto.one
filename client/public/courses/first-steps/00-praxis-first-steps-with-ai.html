Tutto.What it is · 1 of 2
Step 1 · behind the curtain

It writes one word at a time.

Claude is not a search engine looking answers up. It predicts the next word, again and again, from patterns learned across a mountain of text.

Your phone keyboard has done a small version of this for years. Claude is the same idea at enormous scale: pick a likely next word, then the next, until an answer exists. That one fact explains most of what follows.

03
Tutto.What it is · 2 of 2
Step 2 · where the manners come from

It read a lot, then it was taught to help.

Two stages. First it learns to predict text from billions of examples. Then people rate its answers until it behaves like an assistant.

The politeness and the caution are trained in, not magic. The same training leaves habits worth knowing: it tends to agree with you, and it tends to write long. You can ask it to disagree, and ask it to be brief.

04
Tutto.What to trust · 1 of 2
Step 3 · calibration, not faith

Strong on familiar work. Check the precise claims.

Because it predicts what sounds right, it is excellent at work the world has seen a million times, and it can state a wrong fact with full confidence.

People call the confident wrong fact a hallucination. You don't need to fear it, you need a habit: verify the specifics, ask for sources, or turn on web search. Be most careful outside your own field, where you can't spot the miss.

05
Tutto.What to trust · 2 of 2
Step 4 · why it "forgets"

It has a working memory, and it fills up.

Everything Claude is paying attention to sits in one container. What's in it, it can use. What falls out, it has quietly lost.

Two habits follow. In long conversations, restate what matters instead of trusting page one to survive. And don't assume it remembers last week: corrections don't carry across chats unless you save them somewhere it can see. The container is big, roughly 500 pages, but it is not infinite.

06
Tutto.Working with it · 1 of 6
Step 5 · meet Claude properly

Treat it like a colleague, not a search box.

No special syntax, no magic words. Talk to it the way you'd brief a capable coworker, and give it work with substance.

Same Claude on web, desktop and mobile, and it stays signed-in across them. The step up isn't a better tool, it's a better class of task: bring it the work you'd normally do yourself, not just the things you'd look up.

07
Tutto.Working with it · 2 of 6
Step 6 · if you take one thing away

Good results start with a proper brief.

Claude can't read your mind. A good ask does three things: sets the scene, states the task, gives the rules.

Compare "write an email about the project delay". Same request, no scene, no rules: you get something generic and you blame the tool. Most disappointing answers are under-briefed asks.

08
Tutto.Working with it · 3 of 6
Step 7 · the method

The first answer is a draft, not a verdict.

Working with Claude is a loop: ask, read what comes back, say what to change, repeat. You decide when it's done.

Specific feedback beats vague feedback: "cut the first two paragraphs" lands better than "make it shorter". And if a conversation has gone off the rails, a fresh chat with a better brief is often faster than rescuing the old one.

09
Tutto.Working with it · 4 of 6
Step 8 · the upgrade most people miss

Say what you're trying to achieve, not just what to change.

An instruction can be followed to the letter and still miss your point. Give the goal next to the instruction and the gap closes.

The fix in one line: "Make this shorter. My goal is that the key finding survives a twenty-second skim." Instruction plus goal. It works on people too.

10
Tutto.Working with it · 5 of 6
Step 9 · context is the fuel

Show it the real material.

Don't describe your document to Claude. Hand it over. It reads reports, spreadsheets, images and slides, and answers from what's actually there.

One conversation holds about 500 pages, so "read this 60-page contract and flag what's unusual" is a normal ask, not a special one. The right context turns a generic draft into something that sounds like your work, because it is.

11
Tutto.Working with it · 6 of 6
Step 10 · set it up once

Stop re-explaining yourself every time.

A Project is a workspace where your files and standing instructions live. Every chat inside it starts already briefed.

Claude's memory also picks up your role and preferences across conversations, and you can review or edit what it holds. Between the two, the tenth chat should feel nothing like the first: you've stopped introducing yourself.

12
Tutto.Making it stick · 1 of 4
Step 11 · the judgment call

Decide what stays yours.

Using Claude properly starts before the first prompt: for each piece of work, choose what you keep, what you do together, and what you hand over.

The goal is not to automate everything. The middle bucket is where the value concentrates: you bring the expertise and the stakes, Claude brings speed and range. If you're unsure where a task goes, ask Claude to help you split it. That conversation is free.

13
Tutto.Making it stick · 2 of 4
Step 12 · the professional standard

Three checks before it leaves your desk.

AI-assisted work is still your work. Three quick checks keep it something you can stand behind.

What "telling people" looks like differs by context: a client report, an internal note and a personal email carry different expectations. Know your workplace's line, and when in doubt, say it plainly in a footer line.

14
Tutto.Making it stick · 3 of 4
Step 13 · the horizon

From answers to finished work.

Everything so far happens in a chat window. The same skills carry into tools where Claude works in your actual files and hands back finished documents.

The step up is delegating, not just chatting: "look across the meeting notes, the email thread and the proposal deck, and write me the one-page summary for the exec team." It plans, works the steps, asks before acting, and saves the result into your folder. That's a later session; today's skills are the foundation it stands on.

15
Tutto.Making it stick · 4 of 4
Step 14 · start now, start small

Pick one task this week.

Not ten. One recurring task you already do: the weekly email, the meeting notes, the spreadsheet check. Run it through the loop.

Comparing Claude's output to your own version is the fastest teacher there is: you'll see exactly where it saves you time and where your judgment stays essential. The prompts that work become your library. That's the whole journey, one task at a time.
