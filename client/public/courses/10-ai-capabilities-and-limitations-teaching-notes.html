AI Capabilities and Limitations — teaching notes

Side-by-side notes for 10-praxis-ai-capabilities-and-limitations.html (12 slides). This is the course that builds calibrated trust — the conceptual partner to the hallucination objection, and the machine half of the 4D framework (course 05). Its recurring move: trust it on the boring, well-worn parts; check it on the specific, checkable parts — names, dates, numbers, citations. Written from the deck and 10-ai-capabilities-and-limitations-lessons.txt, against the Praxis teaching library. Method: teaching-notes-method.md.

How to use these notes
  • Guidance, not a script. Teach the person, not the slides. This course is conceptual, so the whole job is to keep dragging each property back to one real task of theirs.
  • Every teaching slide has a Pause, then ask block. Put the question, then stop. Don't fill the silence. If they freeze: "Take your time, type anything when you're ready, including 'I don't know'." Then wait again.
  • By background gives a line per archetype (tutor/scenarios.md). This is the most natural nine-archetype course of the set — everyone needs calibrated trust — so each slide carries a "where it's reliable for me / where I must verify" line for all nine. Reach for the one in front of you; the rest are a palette, not a checklist.
  • The emotional centre of this course is hallucination. Handle it honestly, not defensively (objections.md — hallucinate / make stuff up). The point isn't "it's safe", it's "here is exactly which kind of wrong this is, and exactly what to do about it".
  • Hold the voice (train-the-trainer.md Part 4): no "great question", no emoji, one term — Claude Code. Underclaim. Cost is "about twenty dollars a month" and stop.

What AI can do, where it breaks, and how to tell which

The idea

Set the frame: this is a small, clear model of the machine. Nine ideas, one per screen. By the end they won't have memorised every failure; they'll be able to look at a wrong answer and name which kind of wrong it is.

Say it like this

"Most people swing between trusting AI completely and not trusting it at all. Both are wrong. By the end of this you'll have a small model of the machine in your head: four things it's strong and weak at, and a way to tell, for any task, how hard you need to check. Nine ideas, one screen each. The watching is the lesson, so I'll take the silences."

Open with

The task they named in intake. "We'll keep coming back to that one task and ask where it's safe to lean on AI, and where you'd have to check."

Lesson 1

Two halves of one system

The idea

The 4D framework (course 05) teaches how you work with AI. This course teaches how AI works with you — the machine properties those human habits respond to. They're two halves of one system, and the properties stay stable even as models improve, so learning the shape pays off long after today's model is retired.

Say it like this

"If you've done the 4D framework, that taught you the human side — how to delegate, describe, judge, own the result. This is the other half: what the machine is actually doing on the far side of those habits. And the reason it's worth your time — the specific models change every few months, but these four properties don't. Learn the shape once and it keeps paying off." (Pairs with framings.md — Claude understands English, the mechanism is stable; here we're naming the mechanism.)

Lesson 2

Four properties, each a continuum

The centrepiece. If they take one thing away, it's this.

The idea

AI isn't uniformly clever or uniformly unreliable. It's strong and weak along four predictable axes — Next Token Prediction, Knowledge, Working Memory, Steerability — and the same mechanism gives you both ends of each. Calibrated trust isn't trusting or distrusting AI wholesale; it's locating your task on each continuum, then matching how hard you check to where it sits.

Say it like this

"Here's the whole course on one picture. Four properties, and each is a slider, not a switch. On the left, the capability end — trust it. On the right, the limitation end — verify more. The mistake everyone makes is treating AI as one thing you either trust or don't. It isn't. The same task can sit far left on one slider and far right on another. The skill is locating your task on each, then checking as hard as where it sits demands." (The core framing for the whole course — keep coming back to "where does this task sit on the slider?".)

Pause, then ask
  • "Take the task you named. Where would you put it — trust it, spot-check it, or verify every line?"
  • "What does it mean to you that the same machine can be reliable and unreliable on the same job, depending on what you ask?"

(wait)

By background (where it's reliable for me / where I must verify)
Investigator
trust it to structure and summarise the file; verify every name, date, and amount before it leaves the building.
Analyst
trust it to build the table shape; verify every figure and that "no data" is honest, not guessed.
Designer
trust it to generate volume and variants; verify specs, brand colours, and any compliance footer.
Knowledge worker
trust it to read and reformat; verify the extracted numbers and that blanks stayed blank.
Reporter
trust it to consolidate and shape; verify attribution and that nothing was smoothed over.
Content
trust it for fluency and structure; verify any stat, name, or claim it slips in.
Form-filler
trust it to allocate the obvious ones; verify edge cases and that totals reconcile.
Outreach
trust it to personalise at volume; verify every number against the customer file.
Exec
trust it to synthesise; verify the load-bearing figures and decisions before the board.
Watch for

"So I have to check everything anyway?" No — that's the point of calibration. You check harder where the slider is right, lighter where it's left. Checking everything equally is as wasteful as checking nothing. (patterns.md — the 80/20 expectation: the last ten percent is yours, and this slide is how you spend it well.)

Lesson 3

Character is trained in, not emergent

The idea

Politeness, helpfulness and caution aren't magic. Two training stages build them — pretraining (a document completer that just predicts the next word) then fine-tuning on human preference (which turns it into a helpful, honest, harmless assistant). Each stage leaves predictable fingerprints: sycophancy, verbosity, over-caution, loose confidence (sounds sure ≠ is right).

Say it like this

"Its personality was trained in, in two stages. First it just learned to predict the next word across a vast amount of text — a document completer with no idea of helping anyone. Then humans rated good answers, and that shaped it into an assistant. That second stage leaves fingerprints: it agrees with you too easily, it over-explains, it hedges when it doesn't need to, and — the important one — it sounds exactly as confident when it's wrong as when it's right. Once you can name those, you can see them coming."

Pause, then ask
  • "Have you ever had AI just agree with you when you half-hoped it would push back? That's a fingerprint with a name."
  • "Where in your work would 'it sounds confident' be most dangerous if you took it at face value?"

(wait)

By background (the fingerprint that bites them most)
Investigator
loose confidence — it states an invented date as surely as a real one.
Analyst
sycophancy — it'll validate your hypothesis instead of testing it.
Designer
verbosity — three paragraphs of rationale when you wanted three options.
Knowledge worker
loose confidence — a guessed field looks identical to a read one.
Reporter
sycophancy — it frames a wobbly week the way you led it to.
Content
verbosity — it pads, and over-caution waters down a sharp line.
Form-filler
loose confidence — a wrong allocation stated with full certainty.
Outreach
sycophancy — it tells you the pitch is great when it's generic.
Exec
loose confidence — a confident projection dressed as a fact in your brief.
Watch for

The live demo of sycophancy lands hard: preface a request with a wrong assumption ("I think this plan is bulletproof") and watch it validate you. If the room is up for it, do it once on their own example. The honest counter, from the start: this is why you explicitly invite pushback — "disagree with me if I'm wrong" changes the answer.

Lesson 4

It's autocomplete at scale (Next Token Prediction)

The idea

AI writes word by word, picking from what tends to follow what (sampling). That single mechanism gives you both the fluency and the fabrication — you can't have one without the other. Fabrication concentrates in specifics: names, dates, statistics, citations, URLs, quotes. The more precise the claim, the more it warrants a check.

Say it like this

"Under the hood, it's autocomplete at scale. It writes the next word based on what usually follows, then the next, then the next. The phone-keyboard game — keep tapping the suggested word and you've built a tiny version of this. Here's the part that matters: the fluency you love and the made-up facts you fear are the same mechanism. You don't get one without the other. And the made-up stuff clusters in one place — the specifics. Names, dates, numbers, citations. The vaguer the claim, the safer; the more precise, the more it earns a check." (This is the honest core of the hallucination answer — objections.md hallucinate / make stuff up: it doesn't know a fact, it predicts a plausible one.)

Pause, then ask
  • "Where in your work do you rely on AI for something specific and checkable — a number, a date, a citation?"
  • "What would it cost you if one of those was confidently, fluently wrong?"

(wait — this is the slide where the hallucination fear is most live. Let them name it.)

By background (the specific that must be checked)
Investigator
a cited judgment date or a company number — the well-worn summary is fine; the specific gets verified against source.
Analyst
an exact figure or a percentage — trust the structure, never the unsourced number.
Designer
a pixel dimension or a regulatory line lifted from a brief — confirm against the spec sheet.
Knowledge worker
a VAT number or invoice total — the format is safe, the digits aren't.
Reporter
who said what, and when — attribution is exactly where it'll fabricate.
Content
a statistic or a quote it dropped in to sound authoritative — check or cut.
Form-filler
a threshold or an allowance — these change, and it'll state an old one confidently.
Outreach
a price, a stat, a case-study number — only what's in the file, nothing invented.
Exec
a market-size figure or a competitor number — load-bearing specifics get sourced.
Watch for

The fix isn't "hope it's right". Name the four levers that push fabrication out: ask for citations, ask it to flag uncertainty, constrain the output (give it the source to quote from), and generate-then-verify. In Claude Code this becomes a standing rule in the skill file: "cite a source for every claim, never infer" (objections.md — hallucinate; the skill file makes verification a default, not a vigilance task).

Lesson 5

It knows what it read, frozen at a cutoff (Knowledge)

The idea

No live browsing by default, no lived experience, a hard stop at the training cutoff. The real question isn't "does it know this?" but "how well-represented was this in what it read?" Capability: mainstream, popular, stable topics. Limitation: rare, post-cutoff, niche, local, contested. Web search, retrieval/RAG and tools breach the cutoff wall.

Say it like this

"It only knows what it read during training, and that reading stopped on a fixed date. No memory of anything since, unless you give it a way to look. So the question to ask yourself isn't 'does it know this?' — it's 'how common and how settled was this in what it read?' Mainstream and stable, it's strong. Recent, niche, local, or anything that changed last month, it's thin — and it won't always tell you it's thin. Web search and connected tools are how you breach that wall when you need current facts."

Pause, then ask
  • "What in your field changed in the last year that a model trained before then would get wrong?"
  • "Where do you have to bring the knowledge yourself, because no general model would have it?"

(wait)

By background (where it's reliable / where the cutoff or niche bites)
Investigator
reliable on legal concepts and process; thin on a specific recent filing — bring the document.
Analyst
reliable on standard methodology; stale on this quarter's rates — supply them.
Designer
reliable on design principles; behind on the latest platform spec — paste it.
Knowledge worker
reliable on common formats; ignorant of your suppliers' quirks — give the context.
Reporter
reliable on general framing; knows nothing of your team's week — that's your input.
Content
reliable on the craft; behind on current events and trends — feed it the sources.
Form-filler
reliable on the mechanics; wrong on this year's thresholds — check the current figure.
Outreach
reliable on industry generalities; blank on this customer — that lives in the file.
Exec
reliable on frameworks; stale on the market right now — pair it with research.
Watch for

The "default assumption" trap is subtle: it treats the outsider's view of your field as normal, because that's what dominated its training data. It'll describe a "typical customer" or "standard case" that isn't yours. Name it so they catch it. (Ties to slide 6: the cure for both staleness and a fabricated specific is the same — give it the source, or send it to look.)

Lesson 6

The middle of a long context gets lost (Working Memory)

The idea

Everything the AI attends to lives in a fixed window. Attention weights the edges, so a key fact buried in the middle of a long context quietly drops out — accuracy falls 30%+ (the "lost in the middle" U-curve). It's a cliff, not a gradient: it works until it doesn't, and the overflow is silent. The fix isn't more context, it's smarter — front-load, repeat what matters, start fresh. Memory, compaction and projects push the cliff out.

Say it like this

"Everything it's paying attention to right now lives in a fixed-size workspace. Think of cramming for an exam — you remember the start and the end of the list, and the middle vanishes. AI does exactly the same. Bury an instruction in the middle of a long document and there's a real chance it's quietly ignored, and you won't get a warning. So the instinct to paste in everything backfires — every extra page pushes something else into the dead zone. The fix isn't more context, it's smarter context: put what matters up front, repeat it at the end, and when a conversation gets long and muddled, start fresh."

Pause, then ask
  • "Have you had a long chat where it seemed to forget something you said earlier? That's this."
  • "What's the one instruction in your task that absolutely cannot be the thing it drops?"

(wait)

By background (where the window bites, and the discipline)
Investigator
a 200-page bundle — front-load the rules, don't trust a buried clause to be read.
Analyst
a long methodology doc — put the comparison rule first and last.
Designer
a brief stuffed with detail — surface the must-haves; appendix the rest.
Knowledge worker
a hundred files at once — that's a job for a script, not one long chat.
Reporter
a fortnight of updates pasted in — the format spec goes top and bottom.
Content
a long style guide — lead with the three rules that matter most.
Form-filler
a fat template — the "never drop a line" rule sits where it can't be lost.
Outreach
a sprawling knowledge base — point it at the relevant section, not the whole thing.
Exec
a full board pack — tell it what the brief is for up front, then again at the end.
Watch for

This is the cleanest place to plant why Claude Code beats the browser: a chat forgets the beginning once it gets long; a folder doesn't (framings.md — context window, why this beats chat; the folder is the memory). And note the hard edge — unlike the other three properties, this one is a cliff. It works fine right up until it silently doesn't.

Lesson 7

It follows the letter, not the spirit (Steerability)

The idea

The model follows instructions the way it does everything — by continuing a pattern, not by understanding. That makes it genuinely steerable on short, concrete, verifiable instructions (format, length, role), and leaves a gap between what you said and what you meant on long reasoning chains, abstract asks, and arithmetic. When it follows you literally but uselessly, restate the goal — saying it louder won't help.

Say it like this

"It's steerable — tell it 'three columns, under 100 words, second person' and it nails it. But it follows your words by pattern-matching, not by understanding what you're after. So 'make it shorter' gets you fewer words, which isn't what you meant — you meant 'surface the ask so a busy exec catches it'. When it does the letter but misses the point, don't repeat the instruction louder. State the goal: 'shorter, so the decision is obvious on page one.' And on anything multi-step or numerical, drop in a checkpoint — a small early slip compounds all the way to the end."

Pause, then ask
  • "When's the last time it did exactly what you asked and still wasn't what you wanted?"
  • "For your task, what's the goal behind the instruction — the thing you'd have to say out loud for it to land right?"

(wait)

By background (the letter-vs-spirit gap, and the goal to state)
Investigator
"summarise this" → goal: "flag what's missing and unverified, not just what's there".
Analyst
"compare these" → goal: "only on the same basis; name it where you can't".
Designer
"make it pop" → goal: name the emotion and the audience, not the adjective.
Knowledge worker
"clean this up" → goal: "into this exact schema, blanks left blank".
Reporter
"make it concise" → goal: "so leadership sees the blocker first".
Content
"make it punchier" → goal: "match the voice in these two past posts".
Form-filler
"sort these" → goal: "one section each, the more specific wins, reconcile totals".
Outreach
"personalise it" → goal: "to this customer's stated problem, from their file".
Exec
"make it executive" → goal: "decision and owner on page one, detail behind it".
Watch for

Arithmetic and long logic chains are the weak spot — it can be confidently wrong at maths. The fix is to offload it: ask it to write code to do the calculation rather than do it in its head. That's the bridge to Claude Code — the script does the deterministic part exactly the same every time (framings.md — deterministic vs probabilistic: Claude understands the English, the code does the work).

Lesson 8

Most failures are two properties meeting

The idea

Real-world trouble is rarely one property acting up — it's usually two colliding. Next Token Prediction + Knowledge = hallucinated specifics (fix: verify names, dates, citations). Working Memory + Steerability = long-conversation drift (fix: re-supply context, restate the goal). Name the pair and the fix points itself out. This is Discernment applied: you evaluate better when you know what kind of wrong you're looking at.

Say it like this

"Most real failures aren't one thing going wrong — they're two. A made-up citation is Next Token Prediction (it generates something plausible) meeting Knowledge (it didn't actually have the source). Drift in a long conversation is Working Memory (it lost the early context) meeting Steerability (so your goal faded). The useful move: when something's off, name the two properties that collided. The fix is almost always written into the pair — verify the specifics, or re-supply the context and restate the goal."

Pause, then ask
  • "Think of a time AI genuinely let you down. Which two of the four were colliding?"
  • "Knowing the pair, what would you have done differently?"

(wait)

By background (the collision they'll hit, and the fix)
Investigator
hallucinated specifics in a report → verify every name, date, citation against source.
Analyst
confidently wrong arithmetic (NTP + Steerability) → offload the maths to code.
Designer
drift across a long brief → re-state the must-haves, work in shorter passes.
Knowledge worker
a guessed field stated confidently → the "leave blanks blank" rule, enforced.
Reporter
misattribution after a long paste → re-supply the roster, cite who said what.
Content
a fabricated stat in fluent prose → demand a source or cut the claim.
Form-filler
a stale threshold applied with certainty → check the current figure, then allocate.
Outreach
an invented number in a personalised draft → only numbers from the file.
Exec
an agreeable bad premise (sycophancy + NTP) → invite genuine pushback explicitly.
Watch for

This is the slide that turns fear into method. "What if it makes things up?" stops being a reason to distrust the whole thing and becomes "that's NTP meeting Knowledge, and here's the two-part fix" (objections.md — hallucinate: the skill file says cite a source or say so, and the script enforces the rule the AI can't be trusted to enforce alone).

Lesson 9

Hold a small model of the machine

The idea

Fluent use isn't memorising every failure mode — it's recognising which kind of wrong you're looking at and reaching for the matching response. Something feels off → run it through the four properties → reach for the matching fix (verify, re-supply, checkpoint). The boundaries move outward with every model upgrade; the four questions don't. This course is the machine half; the 4D framework (course 05) is the human half that acts on it.

Say it like this

"Here's the whole thing as one habit. Something feels off in an answer. Instead of just distrusting it, run it through four questions. Where did this come from — Next Token Prediction? Did it actually know this — Knowledge? Was it paying attention to the right thing — Working Memory? Did I control it well — Steerability? Locate the task, and the fix follows: verify, re-supply the context, or add a checkpoint. That's calibrated trust. And it survives every upgrade — the lines keep moving outward as models get better, but the four questions stay exactly the same."

Pause, then ask
  • "Of the four questions, which one do you think you'll reach for most in your work?"
  • "How does it change things to know the questions outlast the models?"

(wait — this is the consolidation; let them say it back in their own words.)

By background (the question they'll lean on most)
Investigator
"did it actually know this?" — Knowledge, every cited fact.
Analyst
"where did this number come from?" — Next Token Prediction.
Designer
"am I controlling this well?" — Steerability, on abstract briefs.
Knowledge worker
"where did this come from?" — across a hundred files, that's the script.
Reporter
"is it attending to the right updates?" — Working Memory.
Content
"did it know this, or generate it?" — Knowledge meets NTP on any claim.
Form-filler
"did I control this precisely enough?" — Steerability on the rules.
Outreach
"is this number real or generated?" — NTP meets Knowledge.
Exec
all four, fast — it's the brief you put your name on.
Watch for

Close the loop to the 4D framework explicitly: this course is the machine half, 05 is the human half. If they've done 05, this is the why under Discernment. If they haven't, point them there next. And end on the durable claim: the single most valuable thing they leave with is a model that doesn't expire when the next version ships.
